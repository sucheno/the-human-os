Sleep and hormones are not merely related — they are bidirectionally dependent. Hormones regulate when and how well you sleep, and sleep determines when and how much of certain hormones your body produces. Disrupt one, and you inevitably disrupt the other.

This article connects the hormonal knowledge from Section 2 with the sleep architecture and circadian rhythm you have just learned about.

## Growth hormone: the deep-sleep hormone

Growth hormone (GH) is released in pulsatile bursts throughout the day, but the largest pulse — accounting for about 70% of daily GH secretion — occurs during the first episode of N3 (deep) sleep, typically within the first 90 minutes of falling asleep.

GH promotes:

- Tissue repair and muscle protein synthesis
- Fat breakdown (lipolysis)
- Bone growth and maintenance
- Immune cell production

If you delay sleep or reduce total sleep time, you disproportionately lose deep sleep and therefore reduce GH release. This is one mechanism by which chronic sleep deprivation impairs physical recovery and body composition.

## Cortisol: the morning alarm

As covered in the hormones section, cortisol follows a diurnal rhythm — lowest around midnight and highest around 30 minutes after waking (the cortisol awakening response). This rhythm is driven by the circadian clock (SCN) via the HPA axis.

Healthy sleep maintains this rhythm. Sleep deprivation disrupts it in several ways:

- **Elevated evening cortisol:** Even a single night of poor sleep can result in cortisol levels that are still elevated in the evening, when they should be at their lowest. This makes the next night's sleep harder to initiate, since cortisol and melatonin are inversely related.
- **Flattened cortisol curve:** Chronic sleep restriction can flatten the normal cortisol rhythm — levels stay moderately elevated throughout the day rather than showing the healthy peak-and-trough pattern. A flattened cortisol curve is associated with fatigue, insulin resistance, and immune suppression.

This creates a vicious cycle: poor sleep raises cortisol, which makes the next night's sleep worse, which further elevates cortisol.

## Melatonin: the sleep gate

Melatonin's role in signaling sleep timing was covered in the hormones section. The critical interaction here is how sleep itself affects melatonin:

- Melatonin facilitates sleep onset but does not maintain sleep through the night. Its levels peak around 2-4 AM and decline regardless of whether you are still sleeping.
- Light exposure during sleep (from devices, uncovered windows, or nightlights) can suppress melatonin mid-sleep, potentially fragmenting sleep architecture.
- Sleep deprivation itself does not appear to directly reduce melatonin production, but the behaviors associated with staying up late (screen use, bright light exposure) do.

## Insulin and glucose regulation

Sleep has a profound effect on blood sugar control. Research has demonstrated that:

- **After just 4-5 nights of sleep restriction (4 hours per night), healthy young adults showed a 40% reduction in glucose tolerance** — a level comparable to the early stages of prediabetes. Their cells became less responsive to insulin, and the pancreatic beta cells could not fully compensate.
- Slow-wave sleep (N3) appears to be particularly important for insulin sensitivity. Experimentally suppressing deep sleep — without reducing total sleep time — is sufficient to impair glucose tolerance.
- Sleep deprivation increases sympathetic nervous system activity, which promotes glucose production by the liver and reduces peripheral glucose uptake.

These findings explain part of the epidemiological association between short sleep duration and increased risk of type 2 diabetes.

## Leptin and ghrelin: appetite after bad sleep

As discussed in the hunger hormones article, sleep deprivation shifts the appetite hormone balance unfavorably:

- **Leptin drops** — the satiety signal weakens.
- **Ghrelin rises** — the hunger signal strengthens.
- **Appetite for high-calorie, carbohydrate-rich foods increases** — possibly mediated by changes in reward-related brain activity.

A well-cited study found that sleeping only 4 hours for two nights resulted in an 18% decrease in leptin and a 28% increase in ghrelin, accompanied by a 24% increase in appetite — with the strongest cravings for sweets, salty snacks, and starchy foods.

This hormonal shift is one reason why people who sleep poorly tend to eat more, and why sustained sleep deprivation is an independent risk factor for weight gain.

## Thyroid hormones

TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) secretion follows a circadian pattern, peaking in the evening and early night. Sleep itself suppresses TSH — this is normal and part of the feedback system.

Sleep deprivation elevates TSH levels, potentially increasing thyroid hormone production. Chronic sleep disruption may contribute to subtle thyroid axis dysregulation, though this is less well-studied than the other hormonal effects.

## Reproductive hormones

- **Testosterone:** In men, testosterone production is strongly linked to sleep. Most daily testosterone is produced during sleep, and levels begin rising at sleep onset, peaking during the first REM episode. Studies have shown that restricting sleep to 5 hours per night for one week reduced daytime testosterone levels by 10-15% in young healthy men — a magnitude of change equivalent to 10-15 years of aging.
- **Estrogen and progesterone:** Fluctuations in these hormones across the menstrual cycle affect sleep quality. Progesterone has sedative properties (acting on GABA receptors), and its decline just before menstruation may contribute to premenstrual insomnia. During perimenopause, hormonal instability is a major contributor to sleep disruption.

## The compound effect

The power of sleep's hormonal effects is in their simultaneity. One night of poor sleep does not just raise cortisol or lower leptin in isolation — it shifts multiple hormonal systems at once:

- Higher cortisol → higher blood sugar → higher insulin demand
- Lower leptin + higher ghrelin → increased appetite → higher calorie intake
- Lower growth hormone → impaired recovery → reduced exercise capacity
- Higher inflammatory markers → suppressed immune function

These effects compound. Chronically poor sleep does not produce a single hormonal problem — it creates a cascade of interacting disruptions that accelerate metabolic dysfunction.
