Fat-soluble vitamins — A, D, E, and K — dissolve in fat rather than water. This means they are absorbed along with dietary fat in the small intestine, transported through the lymphatic system, and stored in the liver and adipose tissue. Because they can accumulate in the body, both deficiency and excess are possible, though deficiency is far more common.

## Vitamin A

Vitamin A exists in two main forms: preformed vitamin A (retinol), found in animal products, and provitamin A carotenoids (particularly beta-carotene), found in colorful fruits and vegetables.

### Functions

- **Vision:** Retinal, a form of vitamin A, is a component of the photopigment rhodopsin in the retina. Without adequate vitamin A, rod cells cannot function properly, leading to night blindness — one of the earliest signs of deficiency.
- **Immune function:** Vitamin A supports the integrity of epithelial tissues (skin, gut lining, respiratory tract) that serve as barriers against infection. It also influences the production and function of white blood cells.
- **Cell growth and differentiation:** Retinoic acid regulates gene expression involved in cell development.

### Sources

Retinol: liver, dairy products, eggs, fortified foods. Beta-carotene: sweet potatoes, carrots, spinach, kale, cantaloupe.

### Recommended intake

The RDA for adults is 900 micrograms retinol activity equivalents (RAE) for men and 700 mcg RAE for women. Excessive intake of preformed vitamin A (typically from supplements or liver) can cause toxicity, including liver damage and birth defects during pregnancy. Beta-carotene from foods does not cause toxicity — the body regulates its conversion to retinol.

## Vitamin D

Vitamin D is unique among vitamins because the body can synthesize it when skin is exposed to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation from sunlight. Despite this, deficiency is widespread — an estimated 35% of adults in the United States have insufficient levels, according to NIH data.

### Functions

- **Calcium absorption:** Vitamin D's most critical role is promoting calcium absorption in the small intestine. Without adequate vitamin D, the body absorbs only about 10-15% of dietary calcium; with adequate vitamin D, that rises to 30-40%.
- **Bone health:** By regulating calcium and phosphorus levels, vitamin D is essential for bone mineralization. Severe deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia (softened bones) in adults.
- **Immune modulation:** Vitamin D receptors are present on many immune cells, and vitamin D appears to support both innate and adaptive immune function.
- **Muscle function:** Deficiency is associated with muscle weakness, particularly in older adults.

### Sources

Sunlight exposure (10-30 minutes of midday sun on unprotected skin, depending on latitude, skin pigmentation, and season), fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), fortified milk and cereals, egg yolks, cod liver oil.

### Recommended intake

The RDA is 600 IU (15 mcg) for adults up to age 70 and 800 IU (20 mcg) for adults over 70. Many researchers and clinicians consider these levels insufficient, particularly for people with limited sun exposure, darker skin, or who live at higher latitudes.

> **What's still debated:** The optimal blood level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D is contested. The Institute of Medicine considers 20 ng/mL sufficient for bone health. The Endocrine Society and some researchers argue that 30 ng/mL or higher is preferable, especially for immune and muscle function. The evidence for vitamin D supplementation reducing risk of cancer, cardiovascular disease, or respiratory infections remains mixed.

## Vitamin E

Vitamin E is primarily an antioxidant — it protects cell membranes from oxidative damage by neutralizing free radicals, which are unstable molecules produced during normal metabolism and in response to environmental stressors.

### Functions

- **Antioxidant protection:** Vitamin E is the primary fat-soluble antioxidant in cell membranes. It is particularly important in protecting the polyunsaturated fatty acids in membranes from oxidation.
- **Immune function:** Vitamin E supports immune cell signaling and function, particularly in older adults.
- **Anti-inflammatory effects:** It helps regulate inflammatory pathways.

### Sources

Sunflower seeds, almonds, hazelnuts, spinach, avocado, wheat germ oil, and other vegetable oils.

### Recommended intake

The RDA for adults is 15 milligrams (22.4 IU) of alpha-tocopherol per day. Deficiency is rare in healthy individuals but can occur with fat malabsorption disorders. High-dose supplementation (above 400 IU/day) has not shown clear benefits and may increase bleeding risk.

## Vitamin K

Vitamin K exists in two main forms: K1 (phylloquinone), found in green leafy vegetables, and K2 (menaquinones), produced by gut bacteria and found in fermented foods, meat, and dairy.

### Functions

- **Blood clotting:** Vitamin K is essential for the synthesis of several clotting factors in the liver. Without it, blood cannot clot properly, leading to excessive bleeding. This is vitamin K's best-established function and the reason newborns receive a vitamin K injection at birth (since they are born with low stores and limited gut bacteria).
- **Bone metabolism:** Vitamin K activates osteocalcin, a protein produced by osteoblasts that binds calcium to the bone matrix. Adequate vitamin K intake is associated with better bone mineral density.
- **Cardiovascular health:** Vitamin K activates matrix Gla protein, which helps prevent calcium deposition in blood vessel walls (arterial calcification). Emerging research suggests K2 may be particularly important for this function.

### Sources

K1: kale, spinach, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, lettuce. K2: natto (fermented soybeans), hard cheeses, egg yolks, chicken, butter.

### Recommended intake

The adequate intake (AI) for adults is 120 micrograms per day for men and 90 mcg for women. Note for people on blood-thinning medication (warfarin): vitamin K intake should be consistent rather than varied, since warfarin works by antagonizing vitamin K's role in clotting.
