Minerals are inorganic elements that the body cannot synthesize. They must come from food (or water), and they play structural, regulatory, and catalytic roles in virtually every body system. Unlike vitamins, minerals cannot be destroyed by heat or light during cooking — though they can be lost through leaching into cooking water.

Minerals are classified by the amount needed: **macrominerals** are required in larger quantities (more than 100 mg/day), while **trace minerals** are needed in smaller amounts.

## Macrominerals

### Calcium

The most abundant mineral in the body — about 99% of it is in bones and teeth, where it provides structural rigidity. The remaining 1% circulates in the blood and is essential for muscle contraction, nerve signal transmission, blood clotting, and enzyme function.

Blood calcium is tightly regulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitonin, and vitamin D. When blood calcium drops, PTH stimulates the release of calcium from bones, increases calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, and activates vitamin D to enhance intestinal absorption.

**RDA:** 1,000 mg/day for most adults; 1,200 mg for women over 50 and adults over 70. **Sources:** dairy products, fortified plant milks, sardines with bones, leafy greens (kale, bok choy), tofu made with calcium sulfate.

### Magnesium

A cofactor in over 300 enzyme reactions, including energy production (ATP synthesis), protein synthesis, muscle and nerve function, and blood glucose regulation. Magnesium also plays a role in sleep quality and stress response by modulating the HPA axis and GABA receptor activity.

Subclinical magnesium deficiency may be more common than recognized — some estimates suggest that up to 50% of the U.S. population does not meet the RDA through diet alone.

**RDA:** 400-420 mg/day for adult men; 310-320 mg for adult women. **Sources:** nuts (almonds, cashews), seeds, legumes, whole grains, spinach, dark chocolate.

### Potassium

Critical for maintaining cell membrane potential, which is essential for nerve impulse transmission and muscle contraction (including the heart). Potassium also helps regulate blood pressure by counteracting the effects of sodium — higher potassium intake is associated with lower blood pressure.

**AI:** 2,600 mg/day for adult women; 3,400 mg for adult men. Most adults in the U.S. consume less than the recommended amount. **Sources:** bananas, potatoes, sweet potatoes, spinach, beans, yogurt, salmon.

### Sodium

Essential for fluid balance, nerve function, and muscle contraction. Sodium is the primary electrolyte in extracellular fluid. The kidneys regulate sodium balance, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system adjusts retention or excretion based on blood volume and pressure.

Sodium deficiency is rare in developed countries — the far more common issue is excessive intake. The Dietary Guidelines recommend limiting sodium to less than 2,300 mg per day, yet the average American consumes about 3,400 mg. Excess sodium intake is associated with elevated blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk.

**Sources:** Table salt, processed foods, bread, canned soups, restaurant meals.

### Phosphorus

Works closely with calcium in bone mineralization — about 85% of the body's phosphorus is in bones and teeth. It is also a component of ATP, DNA, RNA, and cell membranes (phospholipids). Deficiency is uncommon because phosphorus is widespread in the food supply.

**RDA:** 700 mg/day for adults. **Sources:** dairy, meat, fish, poultry, nuts, legumes, grains.

## Trace minerals

### Iron

Essential for oxygen transport. Iron is a central component of hemoglobin (in red blood cells) and myoglobin (in muscle cells). It also plays a role in energy metabolism and immune function.

Iron exists in food in two forms: **heme iron** (from animal sources — more easily absorbed, 15-35% absorption rate) and **non-heme iron** (from plant sources — less efficiently absorbed, 2-20%). Vitamin C significantly enhances non-heme iron absorption.

Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide and the leading cause of anemia, particularly in premenopausal women (due to menstrual blood loss), pregnant women, and young children.

**RDA:** 8 mg/day for adult men and postmenopausal women; 18 mg for premenopausal women. **Sources:** red meat, poultry, fish, lentils, spinach, fortified cereals.

### Zinc

Involved in immune function, wound healing, DNA synthesis, and cell division. Zinc is a cofactor for over 300 enzymes. It also plays a role in taste and smell perception.

**RDA:** 11 mg/day for men; 8 mg for women. **Sources:** oysters (highest concentration of any food), red meat, poultry, beans, nuts, whole grains, dairy.

### Selenium

Functions as a component of selenoproteins, which have antioxidant roles (glutathione peroxidases) and are involved in thyroid hormone metabolism. The thyroid gland contains the highest concentration of selenium per gram of any organ.

**RDA:** 55 mcg/day for adults. **Sources:** Brazil nuts (one to two nuts can provide the daily requirement), seafood, meat, grains.

### Iodine

Essential for thyroid hormone synthesis, as covered in the hormones section. The thyroid gland actively concentrates iodine from the blood.

**RDA:** 150 mcg/day for adults. **Sources:** iodized salt, seaweed, dairy, eggs, seafood.

## Mineral interactions

Minerals do not work in isolation — they interact with each other and with vitamins in ways that affect absorption and function:

- Calcium and magnesium compete for absorption — very high calcium intake can reduce magnesium absorption.
- Iron and calcium compete for absorption when consumed simultaneously.
- Vitamin D enhances calcium absorption.
- Vitamin C enhances iron absorption.
- Zinc and copper compete for absorption — very high zinc supplementation can cause copper deficiency.

These interactions are one reason why obtaining minerals from a varied diet is generally preferable to relying on individual supplements.
