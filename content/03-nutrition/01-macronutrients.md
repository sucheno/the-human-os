Macronutrients are the nutrients your body needs in large quantities — grams per day rather than milligrams. There are three: carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Each provides energy (measured in calories), but they serve very different roles in the body and are metabolized through different pathways.

## Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are the body's preferred quick-access energy source. They are broken down into glucose, which cells use to produce ATP — the universal energy currency.

### Types

- **Simple carbohydrates (sugars):** Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) and disaccharides (sucrose, lactose, maltose). They are absorbed rapidly, causing a faster rise in blood glucose.
- **Complex carbohydrates (starches):** Long chains of glucose molecules found in grains, legumes, and starchy vegetables. They take longer to digest and produce a more gradual glucose rise.
- **Fiber:** A form of carbohydrate that human digestive enzymes cannot break down. Soluble fiber dissolves in water and can slow glucose absorption and lower cholesterol. Insoluble fiber adds bulk to stool and supports healthy bowel function. Adequate fiber intake is associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer.

### Energy yield

Carbohydrates provide approximately 4 calories per gram.

### Storage

The body stores carbohydrates as glycogen — about 100 to 120 grams in the liver and 300 to 400 grams in skeletal muscles. Liver glycogen maintains blood sugar between meals; muscle glycogen fuels physical activity. Total glycogen stores represent roughly 1,600 to 2,000 calories — enough for about a day of normal activity.

When glycogen stores are full and carbohydrate intake exceeds energy needs, the excess can be converted to fat through a process called de novo lipogenesis, though under normal dietary conditions most excess calories stored as fat come from dietary fat itself.

## Protein

Proteins are the body's primary structural and functional molecules. They are assembled from 20 amino acids, of which 9 are essential — the body cannot synthesize them, so they must come from food.

### Functions

- **Structural:** Collagen (connective tissue, skin), keratin (hair, nails), actin and myosin (muscle contraction).
- **Enzymatic:** Nearly all enzymes are proteins. They catalyze biochemical reactions throughout the body.
- **Hormonal:** Many hormones are proteins or peptides (insulin, growth hormone).
- **Transport:** Hemoglobin carries oxygen; albumin carries fatty acids and hormones in the blood.
- **Immune:** Antibodies are proteins.
- **Energy:** Protein can be used for energy (4 calories per gram) but is not the body's preferred fuel source.

### Dietary sources

Complete proteins — containing all 9 essential amino acids in adequate proportions — are found in animal products (meat, fish, eggs, dairy) and a few plant sources (soy, quinoa). Most plant proteins are incomplete individually but can be combined over the course of a day to provide all essential amino acids (e.g., legumes and grains).

### How much

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day for the average sedentary adult. This is a minimum to prevent deficiency. Research suggests that physically active individuals, older adults, and those recovering from illness may benefit from higher intakes — roughly 1.2 to 2.0 grams per kilogram per day, depending on activity level and goals.

## Fat

Dietary fats are the most energy-dense macronutrient at 9 calories per gram. Despite decades of messaging suggesting fat is harmful, dietary fat is essential. It serves as a concentrated energy reserve, insulates organs, forms the core structure of every cell membrane, and is required for absorbing fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

### Types

- **Saturated fat:** No double bonds in the carbon chain. Solid at room temperature (butter, coconut oil, animal fat). Higher intake is associated with elevated LDL cholesterol, though the relationship between saturated fat and cardiovascular disease is more nuanced than once believed.
- **Monounsaturated fat (MUFA):** One double bond. Liquid at room temperature (olive oil, avocados, nuts). Associated with cardiovascular benefits.
- **Polyunsaturated fat (PUFA):** Multiple double bonds. Includes omega-6 (found in vegetable oils, nuts, seeds) and omega-3 (found in fatty fish, flaxseed, walnuts) fatty acids. Omega-3s (EPA and DHA) are particularly important for brain function, reducing inflammation, and cardiovascular health.
- **Trans fat:** Created by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils. Strongly associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Artificial trans fats have been largely eliminated from food supplies in many countries.

### Essential fatty acids

Two fatty acids are essential — the body cannot produce them:

- **Linoleic acid (omega-6):** Found in vegetable oils, nuts, and seeds.
- **Alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3):** Found in flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts, and canola oil. The body can convert ALA to EPA and DHA, but the conversion rate is low (typically less than 10%), which is why direct dietary sources of EPA and DHA (fatty fish) are recommended.

## The energy balance

All three macronutrients provide calories. The total energy balance — calories consumed versus calories expended — determines whether you gain, lose, or maintain weight. But the source of those calories matters for hormonal responses, satiety, nutrient adequacy, and long-term health outcomes.

A meal high in protein tends to suppress ghrelin longer and increase satiety. A meal high in refined carbohydrates without much fiber or protein tends to produce a sharper insulin spike and earlier return of hunger. The macronutrient composition of your diet influences how the hormonal systems you learned about in Section 2 behave throughout the day.
