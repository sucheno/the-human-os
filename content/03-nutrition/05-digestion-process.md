You covered the digestive system's anatomy in Section 1. This article zooms into the process itself — the sequential breakdown of a mixed meal into absorbable molecules. Understanding digestion step by step clarifies why certain food combinations affect energy differently, why some nutrients require specific conditions for absorption, and why digestive discomfort often has identifiable causes.

## Phase 1: Before you eat (cephalic phase)

Digestion begins before food enters your mouth. The sight, smell, or even thought of food triggers the cephalic phase — the vagus nerve signals the stomach to start producing gastric acid and digestive enzymes, and saliva production increases. This is why an appetizing meal makes your mouth water.

This anticipatory response primes the digestive system, improving the efficiency of digestion once food arrives.

## Phase 2: The mouth (mechanical and enzymatic start)

Chewing (mastication) physically breaks food into smaller pieces, increasing the surface area available for enzymes. This step is often underappreciated — thorough chewing genuinely improves digestion.

Saliva contributes two key components:

- **Salivary amylase:** Begins breaking down starch into maltose and shorter glucose chains. If you chew a piece of plain bread long enough, you will notice it starts to taste sweet — that is salivary amylase at work.
- **Lingual lipase:** A fat-digesting enzyme secreted by glands at the back of the tongue. Its contribution is minor compared to pancreatic lipase, but it begins fat breakdown early.

The tongue forms the chewed food into a ball (bolus) and pushes it to the back of the throat, triggering the swallowing reflex.

## Phase 3: The stomach (acid and protein digestion)

The bolus enters the stomach through the lower esophageal sphincter. The stomach environment is hostile by design:

- **Hydrochloric acid (HCl):** Secreted by parietal cells, it drops the pH to 1.5-3.5. This acid denatures proteins (unfolding their three-dimensional structure), kills most ingested bacteria, and activates pepsinogen into pepsin.
- **Pepsin:** The primary stomach enzyme, it cleaves proteins into smaller peptide fragments. Pepsin works optimally in the acidic stomach environment and is inactive at neutral pH.
- **Gastric lipase:** Contributes to fat digestion, though the majority of fat digestion occurs later in the small intestine.
- **Mucus:** Goblet cells produce a thick mucus layer that protects the stomach lining from its own acid. Disruption of this protective layer can lead to gastric ulcers.

The stomach mechanically churns the bolus, mixing it with gastric juices to produce a semi-liquid called chyme. The stomach releases chyme into the small intestine gradually through the pyloric sphincter — a process that takes 2 to 5 hours depending on meal composition. Fat and protein slow gastric emptying, which is one reason high-protein, higher-fat meals keep you feeling full longer than carbohydrate-dominant meals.

## Phase 4: The small intestine (the main event)

The duodenum — the first 25 centimeters of the small intestine — is where the most critical digestive processes converge:

### Pancreatic secretions

The pancreas releases a cocktail of enzymes into the duodenum:

- **Pancreatic amylase:** Continues starch digestion, breaking it down to maltose and other disaccharides.
- **Proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase):** Continue protein digestion, breaking peptides into smaller peptides and individual amino acids.
- **Pancreatic lipase:** The primary fat-digesting enzyme. It breaks triglycerides into monoglycerides and free fatty acids.
- **Bicarbonate:** Neutralizes the acidic chyme from the stomach, raising the pH to about 7-8 — the optimal range for pancreatic enzymes.

### Bile

The liver produces bile, which is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder. When fat enters the duodenum, the hormone cholecystokinin (CCK) signals the gallbladder to contract and release bile.

Bile salts emulsify fat — they break large fat globules into many tiny droplets, vastly increasing the surface area available for pancreatic lipase. Without bile, fat digestion and fat-soluble vitamin absorption are severely impaired.

### Brush border enzymes

The cells lining the small intestine (enterocytes) have their own enzymes on their surface:

- **Maltase, sucrase, lactase:** Break disaccharides into monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) that can be absorbed. Lactose intolerance occurs when lactase production is insufficient to break down the lactose in dairy products.
- **Peptidases:** Break remaining small peptides into individual amino acids.

## Phase 5: Absorption

The jejunum and ileum (the remaining ~5.5 meters of small intestine) are the primary absorption sites. The intestinal wall is maximized for absorption through three levels of folding:

- **Circular folds** in the intestinal wall
- **Villi** — finger-like projections about 0.5-1 mm long
- **Microvilli** — microscopic projections on each epithelial cell (the "brush border")

Together, these structures create a surface area of roughly 32 square meters.

- **Monosaccharides and amino acids** are absorbed through active transport into capillaries in the villi, then travel via the hepatic portal vein to the liver.
- **Fatty acids and monoglycerides** are absorbed into enterocytes, reassembled into triglycerides, packaged into chylomicrons, and released into the lymphatic system before eventually entering the bloodstream.
- **Water-soluble vitamins** are absorbed through various transporter mechanisms.
- **Fat-soluble vitamins** are absorbed along with dietary fat.

## Phase 6: The large intestine (water recovery and fermentation)

What remains — primarily water, electrolytes, fiber, and undigested material — enters the large intestine. Here, most of the remaining water is absorbed (about 1.5 liters per day), and the gut microbiome ferments certain fibers, producing short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that nourish colon cells and may have systemic health effects.

Transit through the colon takes 12 to 36 hours. The end product is feces — about 75% water and 25% solids (fiber, bacteria, shed epithelial cells, and small amounts of fat and protein).
