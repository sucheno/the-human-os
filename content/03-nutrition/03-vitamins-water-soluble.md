Water-soluble vitamins dissolve in water, are absorbed directly into the bloodstream from the small intestine, and are not stored in significant quantities. Excess amounts are generally excreted in urine rather than accumulated. This means you need a relatively steady dietary supply — unlike fat-soluble vitamins, you cannot build up large reserves to draw from during periods of low intake.

## The B vitamins

The B vitamins are a group of eight chemically distinct nutrients that share a common role: they act as coenzymes — helper molecules that enzymes need in order to catalyze specific biochemical reactions. Most B vitamins are involved in energy metabolism, converting food into ATP.

### B1 (Thiamine)

Essential for carbohydrate metabolism. Thiamine pyrophosphate is a coenzyme in the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, a critical step in generating energy from glucose. Severe deficiency causes beriberi (nerve and heart damage) and is seen most commonly in chronic alcohol use disorder, since alcohol impairs thiamine absorption. **Sources:** whole grains, pork, legumes, seeds.

### B2 (Riboflavin)

A component of FAD and FMN, coenzymes involved in the electron transport chain and many oxidation-reduction reactions. Riboflavin also helps recycle other antioxidants like glutathione. Deficiency can cause cracked lips, sore throat, and sensitivity to light. **Sources:** dairy products, eggs, lean meats, green vegetables.

### B3 (Niacin)

A component of NAD and NADP, coenzymes central to hundreds of metabolic reactions including energy production, DNA repair, and cell signaling. The body can synthesize small amounts of niacin from the amino acid tryptophan. Severe deficiency causes pellagra — characterized by dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia. **Sources:** poultry, fish, peanuts, mushrooms, fortified grains.

### B5 (Pantothenic acid)

A component of coenzyme A (CoA), which is essential for fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, and for the citric acid cycle. Deficiency is rare because B5 is found in almost all foods. **Sources:** chicken, beef, potatoes, oats, tomatoes, broccoli.

### B6 (Pyridoxine)

Involved in over 100 enzyme reactions, primarily in amino acid metabolism. B6 is also needed for the synthesis of neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, GABA) and hemoglobin. Deficiency can cause anemia, depression, confusion, and weakened immunity. **Sources:** poultry, fish, potatoes, bananas, chickpeas.

### B7 (Biotin)

A coenzyme for carboxylase enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis, gluconeogenesis, and amino acid metabolism. Biotin is widely marketed for hair and nail health, though evidence for supplementation benefits in people without deficiency is limited. Deficiency is rare. **Sources:** eggs, nuts, seeds, salmon, sweet potatoes.

### B9 (Folate)

Essential for DNA synthesis and cell division. Folate is critically important during pregnancy — inadequate folate increases the risk of neural tube defects in the developing fetus, which is why folic acid (the synthetic form) fortification of grain products is mandated in many countries. Folate also works with B12 to form red blood cells. Deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia. **Sources:** leafy greens, legumes, citrus fruits, fortified grains.

### B12 (Cobalamin)

Required for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and neurological function. B12 is unique among vitamins in that it is found almost exclusively in animal products. The body stores B12 in the liver (enough for several years), so deficiency develops slowly.

Absorption of B12 requires a protein called intrinsic factor, produced by the stomach. Conditions that reduce intrinsic factor or stomach acid (including aging, autoimmune gastritis, and long-term use of acid-reducing medications) can cause B12 deficiency even with adequate dietary intake.

Deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia and neurological symptoms — numbness, tingling, memory problems, and difficulty walking. **Sources:** meat, fish, shellfish, dairy, eggs, fortified plant milks.

## Vitamin C (Ascorbic acid)

Vitamin C is the most well-known water-soluble vitamin, in part because of its historical association with scurvy — the disease that afflicted sailors on long voyages before the role of citrus fruits was understood.

### Functions

- **Collagen synthesis:** Vitamin C is essential for the hydroxylation of proline and lysine in collagen production. Without it, collagen is defective, leading to the connective tissue breakdown that characterizes scurvy — bleeding gums, poor wound healing, bruising, joint pain.
- **Antioxidant:** Vitamin C is a potent water-soluble antioxidant that neutralizes free radicals and regenerates vitamin E.
- **Iron absorption:** Vitamin C enhances the absorption of non-heme iron (the form found in plant foods) by converting ferric iron to its more absorbable ferrous form. Eating vitamin C-rich foods with iron-rich plant foods significantly increases iron uptake.
- **Immune function:** Vitamin C supports various cellular functions of the immune system. It accumulates in phagocytic cells and enhances their ability to kill pathogens.

### Sources

Citrus fruits, strawberries, bell peppers, broccoli, kale, kiwi.

### Recommended intake

The RDA for adults is 90 milligrams per day for men and 75 mg for women. Smokers need an additional 35 mg per day because smoking increases oxidative stress and depletes vitamin C. The upper tolerable limit is 2,000 mg per day — excessive supplementation can cause digestive distress and, in susceptible individuals, kidney stones.

## A note on supplements versus food

For most people eating a varied diet, individual vitamin supplementation is unnecessary. Whole foods provide vitamins in combination with other nutrients, fiber, and phytochemicals that may work synergistically. Supplementation makes sense in specific situations: B12 for vegans, folate during pregnancy, vitamin D for those with limited sun exposure, and in cases of diagnosed deficiency.
