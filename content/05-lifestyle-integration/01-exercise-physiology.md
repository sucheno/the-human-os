Exercise is the single most broad-spectrum intervention available for human health. Regular physical activity improves cardiovascular function, insulin sensitivity, bone density, immune regulation, mood, sleep quality, and cognitive function. It does this not through one mechanism but through dozens of interconnected physiological responses — many of which connect directly to the systems and hormones covered in this course.

## Energy systems during exercise

All movement requires ATP (adenosine triphosphate). The body has three systems for producing it, and which one dominates depends on the intensity and duration of the activity:

### Phosphocreatine system (0-10 seconds)

For very short, explosive efforts — a maximal sprint, a single heavy lift — the body uses stored ATP and creatine phosphate in the muscle. This system provides energy almost instantly but is exhausted in about 10 seconds.

### Anaerobic glycolysis (10 seconds to ~2 minutes)

For sustained high-intensity effort, muscles break down glucose (from blood glucose or stored glycogen) without oxygen. This process is fast but produces lactate as a byproduct. When lactate accumulates faster than the body can clear it, you feel the characteristic burning sensation and fatigue. Contrary to an older myth, lactate itself is not a waste product — it is recycled by the liver and other tissues as fuel.

### Aerobic metabolism (2 minutes onward)

For sustained moderate-intensity activity — jogging, cycling, swimming — the body increasingly relies on aerobic pathways in the mitochondria, using oxygen to fully metabolize glucose and fatty acids into ATP. This system is slower but far more efficient, producing roughly 36 ATP per glucose molecule versus 2 from anaerobic glycolysis.

During prolonged exercise, the body progressively shifts from glucose to fat as its primary fuel source. At moderate intensity, fat can supply the majority of energy needs — this is why aerobic exercise is effective for fat utilization.

## Cardiovascular response

During exercise, the cardiovascular system makes rapid adjustments:

- **Heart rate increases** from a resting rate of 60-80 bpm to as high as 180-200 bpm, depending on age and intensity.
- **Stroke volume increases** — each heartbeat pumps more blood.
- **Cardiac output** (heart rate × stroke volume) can increase from about 5 liters/minute at rest to 20-25 liters/minute during vigorous exercise.
- **Blood is redistributed** — blood flow to working muscles can increase 15 to 20 times above resting levels, while flow to the digestive system and kidneys decreases temporarily. This is why vigorous exercise immediately after eating can cause digestive discomfort.
- **Blood pressure rises moderately** during exercise but returns to baseline (or below) afterward. Regular exercise reduces resting blood pressure over time.

## Hormonal responses

Exercise triggers a coordinated hormonal cascade:

- **Adrenaline and noradrenaline** rise rapidly, increasing heart rate, mobilizing glucose from glycogen, and promoting fat release from adipose tissue.
- **Cortisol** rises during prolonged or intense exercise, helping mobilize energy stores. Brief cortisol spikes from exercise are normal and not harmful — they are resolved quickly, unlike chronic stress elevation.
- **Growth hormone** increases during exercise, particularly with high-intensity resistance training, supporting tissue repair and fat metabolism.
- **Insulin decreases** during exercise, allowing muscles to take up glucose through insulin-independent pathways (primarily via GLUT4 transporters activated by muscle contraction). This is one mechanism by which exercise improves blood sugar control.
- **Myokines** — signaling molecules released by contracting muscles — have widespread effects. IL-6 released during exercise (a different context from chronic inflammatory IL-6) improves insulin sensitivity, stimulates fat breakdown, and has anti-inflammatory effects. Other myokines include irisin (may promote fat browning), BDNF (supports brain health), and IL-15 (influences fat metabolism).

## Adaptation: the training effect

The benefits of exercise come not from a single session but from repeated exposure that drives adaptation:

### Cardiovascular adaptations

- The heart muscle becomes stronger, increasing stroke volume.
- Resting heart rate decreases (endurance athletes often have resting rates of 40-60 bpm).
- Capillary density in muscles increases, improving oxygen delivery.
- Blood vessel endothelial function improves, reducing cardiovascular risk.

### Metabolic adaptations

- Mitochondrial density in muscle cells increases, improving aerobic capacity.
- Insulin sensitivity improves — muscles become more effective at clearing glucose from the blood.
- The body becomes better at using fat as fuel during moderate activity.
- Glycogen storage capacity increases in trained muscles.

### Musculoskeletal adaptations

- Resistance training stimulates muscle protein synthesis, increasing muscle mass and strength.
- Bone density increases in response to mechanical loading (weight-bearing exercise), reducing osteoporosis risk.
- Connective tissue (tendons, ligaments) strengthens with progressive loading.

### Neurological and psychological adaptations

- Exercise promotes the release of endorphins and endocannabinoids, contributing to the "runner's high" and general mood improvement.
- Regular exercise increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), which supports neuroplasticity, learning, and memory.
- It reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety, with effect sizes comparable to some pharmaceutical interventions in mild-to-moderate cases.

## How much and what type

Current physical activity guidelines from the World Health Organization recommend for adults:

- **150-300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity** (brisk walking, cycling, swimming) OR **75-150 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity** (running, high-intensity interval training), OR a combination.
- **Muscle-strengthening activities** involving major muscle groups on 2 or more days per week.

Both aerobic and resistance exercise are important — they produce different but complementary benefits. The most consistent finding across all exercise research is that the biggest health improvement comes from going from no exercise to some exercise. The dose-response curve is steepest at the low end.
