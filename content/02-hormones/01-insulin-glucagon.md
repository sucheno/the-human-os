Your blood glucose level at any given moment is the result of a constant tug-of-war between two hormones produced by the pancreas: insulin, which lowers blood sugar, and glucagon, which raises it. Together they maintain glucose within a remarkably tight range — roughly 70 to 100 milligrams per deciliter in a fasting state — because both too-high and too-low blood sugar are dangerous.

## Why blood sugar matters

Glucose is the primary fuel for the brain, which consumes about 120 grams of it per day — roughly 20% of the body's total energy expenditure despite representing only about 2% of body weight. Unlike muscles, which can burn fatty acids, the brain relies heavily on a steady glucose supply. If blood sugar drops too low (hypoglycemia), confusion, seizures, and loss of consciousness can follow. If it stays too high (hyperglycemia) over time, it damages blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, and eyes.

The body treats blood sugar regulation as a top priority.

## Insulin: the storage signal

Insulin is produced by beta cells in the islets of Langerhans within the pancreas. When you eat a meal containing carbohydrates, blood glucose rises. Beta cells detect this increase and release insulin into the bloodstream.

Insulin acts on cells throughout the body, but its primary targets are:

- **Muscle cells:** Insulin signals them to take up glucose from the blood and either use it immediately for energy or store it as glycogen (a branching chain of glucose molecules).
- **Fat cells (adipocytes):** Insulin promotes the uptake of glucose and its conversion to fatty acids for long-term storage as triglycerides.
- **Liver cells:** Insulin signals the liver to take up glucose and store it as glycogen. The liver can store roughly 100 to 120 grams of glycogen — enough to supply the brain for about a day.

Think of insulin as a "store and use" signal. When insulin is high, your body is in storage mode — putting away fuel from a recent meal.

## Glucagon: the release signal

Glucagon is produced by alpha cells in the same pancreatic islets. When blood glucose falls — between meals, during overnight fasting, or during exercise — alpha cells release glucagon.

Glucagon acts primarily on the liver, signaling it to:

- Break down stored glycogen back into glucose (glycogenolysis) and release it into the bloodstream.
- Produce new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources like amino acids and glycerol (gluconeogenesis).

Glucagon is essentially the opposite of insulin — it mobilizes stored energy rather than storing it. When glucagon is dominant, the body is in a "release and burn" state.

## The insulin-glucagon balance

![Insulin and glucagon working like a seesaw to regulate blood glucose](assets/images/img-02-01-insulin-glucagon-seesaw.jpeg)

In a healthy person, insulin and glucagon rarely act simultaneously at high levels. After a meal, insulin rises and glucagon falls. Between meals, insulin drops and glucagon rises. This seesaw keeps blood glucose stable.

The ratio of insulin to glucagon — not the absolute level of either one — determines whether the body is in storage mode or mobilization mode. This concept is important because it explains why the timing and composition of meals affect energy levels, hunger, and fat storage.

## What happens when the system breaks down

### Insulin resistance

When cells are chronically exposed to high insulin levels — often due to consistently high carbohydrate intake, excess body fat (particularly visceral fat), physical inactivity, or genetic predisposition — they can become less responsive to insulin's signal. This is called insulin resistance.

To compensate, the pancreas produces even more insulin. For a while, this keeps blood glucose normal, but at the cost of persistently elevated insulin. Over time, the beta cells may not be able to keep up. Blood glucose begins to rise, initially causing prediabetes and potentially progressing to type 2 diabetes.

Insulin resistance is not just about blood sugar. Chronically elevated insulin promotes fat storage, increases inflammation, and is associated with elevated blood pressure and abnormal cholesterol levels — a cluster of conditions known as metabolic syndrome.

### Type 1 diabetes

In type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the pancreatic beta cells. Without insulin production, blood glucose rises uncontrollably. Type 1 diabetes requires external insulin administration and is unrelated to lifestyle factors — it is an autoimmune condition.

### Type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes develops gradually from insulin resistance. It is far more common than type 1, accounting for roughly 90-95% of diabetes cases according to the CDC. While genetics play a role, physical inactivity, excess body weight, and dietary patterns are major modifiable risk factors.

## Practical implications

Understanding the insulin-glucagon system helps explain several everyday observations:

- **Why you feel sleepy after a large, carb-heavy meal:** A rapid spike in glucose causes a large insulin release, which can drop blood sugar below baseline within a few hours (reactive hypoglycemia in some individuals), producing fatigue.
- **Why exercise helps blood sugar control:** Muscle contraction can stimulate glucose uptake independently of insulin, reducing the need for high insulin levels.
- **Why protein and fat slow glucose absorption:** Meals that include protein, fat, and fiber cause a slower, more gradual rise in blood glucose, requiring less insulin and producing more stable energy levels.
