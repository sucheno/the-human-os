Melatonin is not a sleeping pill — it is a timing signal. Produced by the pineal gland in the brain, melatonin tells the body that it is dark outside and that it is time to prepare for sleep. It does not knock you out. It opens the gate for sleep by lowering core body temperature, reducing alertness, and shifting the body into its nighttime physiological state.

## How melatonin production works

Melatonin synthesis is controlled by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus — the body's master circadian clock. The process works like this:

1. **Light exposure during the day** keeps melatonin suppressed. Specialized photoreceptor cells in the retina (intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, or ipRGCs) detect light — particularly blue wavelengths around 480 nanometers — and send signals to the SCN.
2. **As evening arrives and light diminishes**, the SCN signals the pineal gland to begin converting serotonin into melatonin. This conversion requires an enzyme called AANAT, whose activity is suppressed by light and activated by darkness.
3. **Melatonin levels rise** about 2 hours before habitual bedtime (a period called the dim light melatonin onset, or DLMO), peak during the middle of the night (typically between 2:00 and 4:00 AM), and decline toward morning.

The key takeaway: melatonin production is driven by the absence of light, not by the presence of darkness per se. Even moderate indoor lighting, particularly from screens, can suppress melatonin release.

## What melatonin does

Melatonin's primary role is to signal nighttime to the body and coordinate the transition to a sleep-promoting state:

- **Lowers core body temperature:** Melatonin promotes vasodilation in the extremities (hands and feet), which helps dissipate heat and drop core temperature. This temperature drop is an important physiological prerequisite for sleep onset.
- **Reduces alertness:** Melatonin acts on MT1 and MT2 receptors in the SCN and other brain regions, promoting drowsiness and reducing the drive for wakefulness.
- **Synchronizes peripheral clocks:** Nearly every organ has its own circadian clock. Melatonin helps synchronize these peripheral clocks with the master clock in the SCN, keeping the body's rhythms coordinated.

Melatonin also has antioxidant properties and may play a role in immune regulation, though these functions are less well-understood and less relevant to its primary role as a circadian signal.

## Light at night: the modern problem

For most of human history, the light-dark cycle was determined by the sun. After sunset, the only light sources were fire and moonlight, neither of which emits much blue light. Melatonin would begin rising naturally as the sky darkened.

Modern indoor lighting and screens have fundamentally changed this. Research has shown that:

- Exposure to typical room lighting in the evening can suppress melatonin production by more than 50%.
- Blue-enriched light from screens (phones, tablets, computers) is particularly effective at suppressing melatonin because of the sensitivity of ipRGCs to blue wavelengths.
- Even relatively low-intensity light (100 to 200 lux — a moderately lit room) can delay melatonin onset.

This does not mean you must sit in darkness all evening. But it does explain why people who use bright screens shortly before bed often report difficulty falling asleep — their melatonin signal has been delayed or attenuated.

## Melatonin supplements

Melatonin is widely available as an over-the-counter supplement in many countries. A few important points:

- **Dosage matters:** Most supplements contain 1 to 10 milligrams, but the body naturally produces much less — roughly 0.1 to 0.3 milligrams during a normal night. Research suggests that lower doses (0.3 to 0.5 mg) may be more physiologically appropriate for adjusting sleep timing, while higher doses can lead to daytime grogginess and may saturate receptors.
- **Timing matters more than dose:** Melatonin is most useful for shifting circadian timing (jet lag, shift work) rather than as a general sleep aid. Taking it 1 to 2 hours before desired bedtime is a common recommendation.
- **It is not a sedative:** Melatonin does not address the underlying causes of insomnia, which are more often related to hyperarousal, anxiety, or poor sleep habits.

> **What's still debated:** Long-term safety data for melatonin supplementation is limited. While short-term use appears safe for most adults, the effects of taking exogenous melatonin nightly for years are not well-studied. Supplement quality also varies — studies have found that the actual melatonin content of supplements can differ from what is stated on the label.

## The melatonin-cortisol relationship

![Melatonin and cortisol follow inverse 24-hour rhythms — as one rises, the other falls](assets/images/img-02-06-melatonin-cortisol-rhythm.jpeg)

Melatonin and cortisol have an inverse relationship over the 24-hour cycle. As melatonin rises in the evening, cortisol falls. As melatonin drops in the early morning, cortisol rises. This reciprocal pattern is central to the body's sleep-wake rhythm.

When this relationship is disrupted — for example, by evening light exposure delaying melatonin or by chronic stress keeping cortisol elevated at night — sleep quality suffers. We will explore this interaction further in the sleep section.
