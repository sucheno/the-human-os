Estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone are often categorized as "sex hormones" or "reproductive hormones," but this framing is misleadingly narrow. All three are present in everyone — regardless of sex — and they influence bone density, cardiovascular health, mood, muscle mass, fat distribution, brain function, and energy levels throughout life.

## All three, in everyone

It is a common misconception that testosterone is a "male hormone" and estrogen a "female hormone." In reality:

- Men produce small but functionally important amounts of estrogen, primarily through the conversion of testosterone by an enzyme called aromatase. Estrogen in men is important for bone health, brain function, and cardiovascular protection.
- Women produce testosterone in the ovaries and adrenal glands. Testosterone in women supports libido, bone strength, muscle mass, and energy.

The difference is in the relative concentrations. Adult men typically have testosterone levels of about 300 to 1,000 nanograms per deciliter, while women typically have about 15 to 70 nanograms per deciliter. Estradiol (the primary form of estrogen) averages about 10 to 40 picograms per milliliter in men, compared to 15 to 350 picograms per milliliter in premenopausal women (varying with the menstrual cycle phase).

## Testosterone

Testosterone is produced primarily in the Leydig cells of the testes in men and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands in women. Its production is controlled by the HPG axis — the hypothalamus releases GnRH, the pituitary releases LH and FSH, and these stimulate the gonads.

### Key effects

- **Muscle mass and strength:** Testosterone promotes protein synthesis in skeletal muscle. This is why men, on average, have greater muscle mass than women.
- **Bone density:** Testosterone contributes to bone mineralization. Low testosterone is a risk factor for osteoporosis in both men and women.
- **Fat distribution:** Testosterone tends to reduce overall body fat percentage and favors a more central (android) fat distribution pattern.
- **Red blood cell production:** Testosterone stimulates erythropoietin production by the kidneys, which increases red blood cell count.
- **Mood and cognition:** Testosterone influences motivation, confidence, and spatial reasoning. Low levels are associated with fatigue, depressed mood, and reduced motivation.
- **Libido:** In both men and women, testosterone is a primary driver of sexual desire.

### Age-related decline

Testosterone levels in men begin to decline gradually starting around age 30 — roughly 1% to 2% per year. This is a normal physiological change, not a disease state, though significant declines can produce symptoms like fatigue, reduced muscle mass, increased body fat, and mood changes.

## Estrogen

"Estrogen" refers to a group of hormones, the most potent and abundant being estradiol (E2). The ovaries are the primary source in premenopausal women. Men and postmenopausal women produce estrogen through aromatase conversion of androgens in fat tissue, bone, brain, and other tissues.

### Key effects

- **Bone health:** Estrogen is critical for maintaining bone density. It inhibits osteoclast activity (the cells that break down bone). The sharp decline in estrogen at menopause is the primary reason postmenopausal women face increased risk of osteoporosis.
- **Cardiovascular protection:** Estrogen has favorable effects on cholesterol — it tends to raise HDL and lower LDL. It also promotes vasodilation and has anti-inflammatory effects on blood vessel walls. This is one explanation for why premenopausal women have lower cardiovascular disease rates than men of the same age.
- **Brain function:** Estrogen receptors are abundant in the brain, particularly in areas involved in memory (hippocampus) and mood regulation. Fluctuations in estrogen levels are linked to mood changes during the menstrual cycle, perimenopause, and postpartum period.
- **Skin and connective tissue:** Estrogen promotes collagen production and skin hydration.
- **Fat distribution:** Estrogen favors fat storage in the hips, thighs, and buttocks (gynoid pattern), as opposed to the abdominal pattern favored by testosterone-dominant profiles.

## Progesterone

Progesterone is produced primarily by the corpus luteum in the ovaries after ovulation, and by the placenta during pregnancy. Its levels are highest in the second half (luteal phase) of the menstrual cycle.

### Key effects

- **Reproductive role:** Progesterone prepares the uterine lining for potential embryo implantation and supports early pregnancy.
- **Central nervous system:** Progesterone and its metabolites have calming, sedative effects. Progesterone acts on GABA receptors in the brain, similar in mechanism to anti-anxiety medications. This may explain increased sleepiness during the luteal phase.
- **Body temperature:** Progesterone raises basal body temperature by about 0.3 to 0.6 degrees Celsius, which is why body temperature tracking can indicate ovulation.
- **Respiratory drive:** Progesterone stimulates breathing, slightly increasing respiratory rate.

## Menopause and andropause

**Menopause** marks the permanent end of menstrual cycles, typically occurring around age 51. Estrogen and progesterone production drops sharply. The transition period (perimenopause) can last several years and is characterized by irregular hormone fluctuations that can cause hot flashes, sleep disturbances, mood changes, and vaginal dryness.

The rapid loss of estrogen at menopause accelerates bone loss (increasing osteoporosis risk) and removes the cardiovascular protective effect, contributing to the rise in heart disease risk in postmenopausal women.

**Andropause** is sometimes used to describe the gradual decline in testosterone in aging men, but unlike menopause, it is not a distinct event — testosterone decreases slowly over decades without an abrupt hormonal shift.
